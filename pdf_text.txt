# **OpenPath Research and Architecture Report: Designing an Adaptive, Low-Bandwidth
Accessible Onboarding and Form-Completion System for Ethiopia**
Building a genuinely accessible onboarding and form-completion platform for **Savanna**
requires moving beyond superficial WCAG checklists and third-party "accessibility overlay"
widgets toward a **schema-driven, multimodal, progressively enhanced form engine**1.
Empirical time-diary research by Lazar et al. (2007) with 100 blind web users found that
participants lost an average of **30.4% of their web browsing time** to poorly labeled form
controls, confusing page layouts, missing alternative text, and dynamic updates that conflict with
screen readers5. Meanwhile, a 2021 study by Deque Systems found that automated testing
engines such as axe-core detect roughly **57% of WCAG issues by volume** (and Google
Lighthouse catches only **30% to 40%**), leaving broken screen-reader state announcements,
keyboard focus traps, and cognitive overload undetected unless verified manually8. In the
Ethiopian context, these barriers are compounded by low-cost Android hardware, intermittent
mobile connectivity, diverse literacy profiles across Amharic (*fidel*), Afaan Oromoo, Tigrinya,
and Ethiopian Sign Language (EthSL), and non-Western demographic structures including
patronymic naming, dual Ethiopian and Gregorian calendars, Region/Zone/Woreda/Kebele
administrative addresses, and witness-based identity verification under the Fayda National ID
Program (NIDP)12.
To succeed as both a reusable open-source React/TypeScript component library and an
embeddable product for third-party institutions, **OpenPath** must decouple **canonical form
semantics and validation logic** from **adaptive presentation and interaction modalities**2. By
compiling a single declarative JSON/TypeScript form schema into native HTML form primitives
enhanced via headless accessibility hooks, OpenPath can dynamically adapt its interface—
switching seamlessly between compact multi-question groups, GOV.UK-style "one-thing-per-
page" question flows, W3C COGA-aligned Easy-Read layouts, and Cboard-inspired symbol and
phrase-bank response boards—without losing user state or breaking NVDA, TalkBack,
keyboard, or switch navigation18. Furthermore, because third-party embedding via Shadow
DOM breaks cross-boundary ARIA ID references (aria-labelledby, aria-describedby, aria-
controls) on older browsers22, OpenPath should deploy its standalone \<script\> integration
using a **Light-DOM Custom Element paired with CSS @scope and @layer isolation** rather
than an isolated Shadow root22.
Finally, our technical investigation into Ethiopian speech technologies demonstrates that
developers cannot assume general-purpose cloud AI APIs support Amharic reliably simply
because am-ET appears in a documentation table26. An evaluation by dataset.et notes that the
Ethio-ASR paper measured vanilla OpenAI Whisper and Meta SeamlessM4T at **over 100%
Word Error Rate (WER)** on Ethiopian languages (inserting more hallucinated words than
spoken) and scored Meta MMS at **52.0% WER** on Amharic26, while an August 2026
benchmark published by Addis AI reported a **147.86% WER** for ElevenLabs v3 in Amharic
text-to-speech28. Consequently, OpenPath must never depend on an external AI API for
baseline accessibility. Instead, it should implement a **four-tier progressive speech

## **Deliverable A: Executive Summary and Core Findings**
An onboarding and registration system is genuinely accessible only when a user can
independently—or with dignified, structured human assistance—understand why information is
requested, input answers using their preferred sensory and motor modality, recover effortlessly
from errors or network drops, and verify their submission without cognitive, linguistic, or
technological exclusion2. Synthesizing evidence from W3C standards (WCAG 2.2 and COGA),
five major government design systems, thirteen assistive products and component libraries, and
six empirical research studies yields seven foundational engineering conclusions for OpenPath:
> 1. **Compliance Floor vs. Usability Ceiling:** Building to **WCAG 2.2 Level AA** (published
October 2023\) is essential and introduces critical form requirements: **2.4.11 Focus Not
Obscured (Minimum)**, **2.5.7 Dragging Movements**, **2.5.8 Target Size (Minimum) (24×24
CSS px)**, **3.2.6 Consistent Help**, **3.3.7 Redundant Entry**, and **3.3.8 Accessible
Authentication (Minimum)**1. However, empirical studies with users with intellectual disabilities
(Karreman et al., 2007\) and blind users (Lazar et al., 2007\) show that technical WCAG
conformance alone does not guarantee user satisfaction or task completion5. True usability
requires combining WCAG 2.2 AA with **W3C COGA (*Making Content Usable for People with
Cognitive and Learning Disabilities*)** design patterns and **44×44 to 48×48 CSS px (\~12
mm)** touch targets validated in empirical touchscreen studies with motor-impaired users20.
> 2. **Assistive Technology Realities in Africa:** Global accessibility guidance frequently
centers on Apple VoiceOver and Freedom Scientific JAWS. However, the WebAIM Screen
Reader User Surveys \#10 (December 2023–January 2024, n=1,539 self-selected respondents)
and \#11 (July–August 2026, n=1,780) reveal a sharp regional divide: while JAWS leads in
North America (55.5% in 2024; 72.5% in 2026), free open-source **NVDA dominates among
respondents in Africa and the Middle East (69.9% primary desktop share in 2024, rising to
83.0% in 2026\)**40, paired with built-in **Google TalkBack** on Android devices43.
Furthermore, **16.7% of Survey \#10 respondents reported multiple disabilities**, including
**5.3% who reported being both deaf/hard-of-hearing and blind**40.

> 3. **Native HTML Over Custom ARIA Widgets:** The GOV.UK, NHS.UK, and VA.gov design
systems demonstrate that native HTML elements (\<input\>, \<fieldset\>, \<legend\>, \<label\>,
\<dl\>) provide far greater cross-screen-reader stability than custom ARIA widgets45. Even
simple dynamic ARIA patterns can fail in production: GOV.UK Frontend's conditional radio
reveal (aria-expanded on \<input type="radio"\>) violates the WAI-ARIA specification and failed
to announce expanded or collapsed states in 2026 tests across JAWS, NVDA, VoiceOver, and
TalkBack8.
> 4. **Adaptive Pacing ("One Thing Per Page" vs. Grouped Sections):** Neither single-question
screens nor long scrolling forms are universally superior. Asking one question per page (with
\<label\> or \<legend\> wrapped inside \<h1\>) reduces cognitive load, working memory
demands, and validation complexity18, whereas closely related inputs (such as passport details
or a three-part Ethiopian name) benefit from same-page grouping12. OpenPath's schema
engine should allow users and institutions to switch between **Step-by-Step Guided Mode (One
Question Per Page)** and **Section-Grouped Mode** dynamically2.
> 5. **Distinguishing Evidence-Supported Dyslexia Interventions from Myths:** Eye-tracking
research with dyslexic readers by Rello and Baeza-Yates (2013, n=48) and reading-rate studies
by Wery and Diliberto (2016) found that specialty fonts like **OpenDyslexic did not produce a
statistically significant improvement in reading speed or accuracy** over clean sans-serif fonts
(Arial, Helvetica, Verdana), and participants in Rello and Baeza-Yates's study significantly
preferred Verdana (p \= 0.002) and Helvetica (p \= 0.020) over OpenDyslexic50. Conversely,
**avoiding italic text** (p \< 0.001), providing **extra-large letter and word spacing** (Zorzi et al.,
2012; WCAG 1.4.12), offering an **18-point font size option** (Rello et al.), and **reducing
pronoun co-references** (Fajardo et al.) demonstrated statistically significant benefits for
reading speed and comprehension51.
> 6. **Ethiopian Localization Beyond Translation:** Transplanting Western form schemas to
Ethiopia causes immediate registration friction. OpenPath must natively support: (a) **Ge'ez
script (*fidel*) rendering** with generous line height; (b) **dual Ethiopian Calendar (EC) and
Gregorian Calendar (GC)** inputs plus **"Year-Only Declared Birthdates"** permitted by the
Fayda NIDP12; (c) **patronymic 3-part naming** (First, Middle/Father's,
Surname/Grandfather's)12; (d) **Region \-\> Zone/Sub-City \-\> Woreda \-\> Kebele** address
structures12; (e) **over 33 alternative identity proofs and Witness-Based Consent
workflows**14; and (f) **Ethiopian Sign Language (EthSL)** video hooks rather than assuming
Deaf Ethiopians have high reading fluency in written Amharic or English16.
> 7. **Offline-First & Assisted Completion:** Because mobile data in Ethiopia is intermittent and
many rural or disabled applicants rely on a family member, neighbor, or Organization of Persons
with Disabilities (OPD) agent, OpenPath should persist form changes locally via IndexedDB
alongside 1-second debounced autosave (adapting VA.gov's SaveInProgress pattern)4 and
include a transparent **"Assisted Completion Mode"** that records helper involvement while
safeguarding applicant consent14.

NVDA or TalkBack at high speech rates has different onboarding needs than an older job
seeker with low vision using screen magnification, or a Deaf applicant from Hawassa whose
primary language is Ethiopian Sign Language (EthSL)16.

### **Deep-Dive Analysis by Disability Domain**
#### **A. Blindness and Low Vision**
Empirical time-diary research with 100 blind web users by Lazar et al. (2007) found that
participants lost an average of **30.4% of their total web use time** working around inaccessible
page layouts, poorly labeled or unlabeled form controls, missing alternative text, and
compatibility conflicts between screen readers and web applications5. When designing
OpenPath, engineers must account for how desktop and mobile screen readers actually parse
and interact with the DOM:
* **Screen Reader Navigation & Heading Hierarchy:** According to the WebAIM Screen Reader
User Survey \#10 (2024, n=1,539), **88.8% of respondents find heading levels (\<h1\>–\<h6\>)
very or somewhat useful**, and **78% of advanced users navigate primarily by headings**
(compared to 47% of beginners), whereas only **3.7% cite ARIA landmarks as their primary
method** for finding content (even though 31.7% use landmarks often or always when
present)42. Therefore, every OpenPath form step must expose a unique, descriptive \<h1\> (or
\<h3\> inside a step indicator context, per VA.gov guidelines)18. On single-question pages,
wrapping the \<label\> or \<legend\> directly inside the \<h1\> (\<h1\>\<label for="full-
name"\>What is your full name?\</label\>\</h1\>) prevents screen readers from redundantly
announcing the heading and then repeating the identical prompt when the user tabs into the
input18.
* **Comparison of NVDA, JAWS, VoiceOver, and TalkBack:**
* **NVDA (Windows, Open Source)** and **JAWS (Windows, Commercial)** separate reading
into **Browse / Virtual Buffer Mode** (where single-letter shortcuts like H jump headings and F
jumps form fields) and **Focus / Forms Mode** (where keystrokes pass directly into the focused
control)44. When a user presses Tab to move from input to input inside a multi-question form,
NVDA and JAWS remain in Focus Mode; any standalone explanatory paragraph placed

===== PAGE 9 =====
between inputs that is not programmatically associated via aria-describedby or
\<fieldset\>/\<legend\> is skipped during Tab navigation46.
* **TalkBack (Android)** and **VoiceOver (iOS/macOS)** rely on touchscreen **Explore by
Touch** (dragging a finger across the screen) and **Linear Swipe Navigation** (swiping right/left
to step through elements)32. As VA.gov documentation highlights, disabled buttons (disabled
attribute) can still be encountered and read aloud by mobile screen readers during touch
exploration without explaining why the button is inactive, leaving users locked out46.
* **Regional Usage Realities:** While WebAIM notes its survey respondents are self-selected
rather than a controlled global census40, the regional contrast across Surveys \#10 (2024) and
\#11 (2026) is striking: JAWS leads in North America (55.5% vs. 24.0% for NVDA in 2024;
72.5% vs. 15.6% in 2026), whereas free open-source **NVDA dominates in Africa and the
Middle East (69.9% vs. 23.3% for JAWS in 2024; 83.0% vs. 13.6% in 2026\)** and Asia (70.8%
in 2024; 77.4% in 2026\)40. Moreover, VoiceOver on desktop was used by only **5.7% of
respondents with disabilities in 2026** (8.2% in 2024\) compared to **21.7% of respondents
without disabilities** (23.7% in 2024), and respondents without disabilities were over four times
more likely to use macOS than respondents with disabilities40. Testing OpenPath exclusively
with macOS VoiceOver would miss critical behaviors affecting Windows users on NVDA/JAWS
with Chrome, Edge, or Firefox (which accounted for 90.7% of primary desktop OS usage in
2026\) and Android users on TalkBack with Chrome8.
* **Focus Management, Live Regions, and Conditional Reveals:**
* When transitioning between steps in a multi-step flow, keyboard focus must move
programmatically to the new step's unique heading (tabindex="-1") so screen readers announce
the new step context immediately34.
* When validation fails on submit, OpenPath must render an **Error Summary** at the top of
\<main\>, prepend "Error: " to \<title\>, and shift keyboard focus directly to the Error Summary
container66.
* **The Conditional Reveal Problem (Evidence from GOV.UK Frontend):** A common pattern
in government forms is revealing a conditional input when a user selects a radio button (e.g.,
selecting "Phone" reveals a "Phone number" field) by placing aria-controls and aria-expanded
on \<input type="radio"\>8. Investigation of alphagov/govuk-frontend Issues \#979, \#1972, and
\#6010 demonstrates that aria-expanded is **not permitted on the radio role** by the WAI-ARIA
specification (triggering an aria-allowed-attr error in axe-core 4.13.0), and 2026 contributor tests
across **JAWS 2026.2603.86 (Chrome 155), NVDA 2026.1.1 (Firefox 157), VoiceOver (Safari
on macOS 26.6 and iOS 26.0), and TalkBack (Chrome 154 on Android 17\)** confirmed that
**none of these screen-reader/browser combinations announced expanded or collapsed states
on GOV.UK radio buttons**8. Earlier tests in Issue \#1972 also showed TalkBack announcing
*"Double tap to collapse"* on a checked radio button—which fails because a user cannot
deselect a radio button by double-tapping48. Furthermore, Issue \#6010 documented that
revealing a nested radio group inside a conditional radio option caused Up/Down arrow keys to
jump across both radio groups and dynamically altered the announced item count from *"1 of

===== PAGE 10 =====
3"* to *"1 of 6"*91. **Engineering Solution for OpenPath:** Never put aria-expanded on \<input
type="radio"\>. Use aria-controls (which ARIA 1.3 notes is sufficient to indicate that checking a
radio reveals additional controls8) only when revealing a single simple input placed immediately
next in the tab order, and route any multi-field or nested-radio branch to a **separate follow-up
Question Page**8.
* **Accessible Autocomplete Debouncing:** In alphagov/accessible-autocomplete, announcing
filtered suggestion counts on every keystroke causes the aria-live region to read outdated
counts and talk over the user's typing65. OpenPath should adopt GOV.UK's **1,400-millisecond
debounce** after the last keystroke before updating its status region (with progressive
enhancement for ariaNotify where supported)65.
* **Accessible Document Capture & OCR:** Uploading identity or educational certificates is a
major hurdle for blind users. Studying **Microsoft Seeing AI** (created with blind Microsoft
engineer Saqib Shaikh) and **Google Lookout** (available on Android 6+ devices with 2 GB+
RAM and deeply integrated with TalkBack) demonstrates the solution: during document capture,
the interface provides real-time spoken positioning feedback (*"Move top left… hold steady…
capturing"*) before running OCR and reading the extracted text aloud43.
* **Low Vision, Magnification, and Reflow:** Low-vision users require strict adherence to
**WCAG 1.4.10 Reflow** (single-column layout at 320 CSS px width without horizontal
scrolling), **1.4.11 Non-text Contrast** (at least 3:1 contrast for input borders and focus
indicators), and **2.4.11 Focus Not Obscured (Minimum)** (applying CSS scroll-margin so
sticky headers or footers never cover focused elements)36. On desktop "Check Your Answers"
pages, GOV.UK recommends a two-thirds screen width layout for short answers so "Change"
action links stay close to their data values inside a screen magnifier's viewport35.
#### **B. Deafness and Hearing Loss**
In Ethiopia, roughly up to **1 million people live with hearing impairments** (Wakuma, 2015,
cited in Ayalew, 2022\)98, represented nationally by the **Ethiopian National Association of the
Deaf (ENAD)**90.
* **Why Captions Are Not a Complete Substitute for Sign Language:** Developers frequently
assume that text captions or written instructions make an interface fully accessible to Deaf
users. Empirical research demonstrates why this assumption fails. **Ethiopian Sign Language
(EthSL)** is a natural visual-manual-gestural language with its own grammatical structures
(incorporating hand shape, movement, location, palm orientation, and facial expressions) and a
**34-handshape manual alphabet representing the Ge'ez alphabet**16. Studies of Deaf
education across Ethiopian towns (Jimma, Nekemte, Addis Ababa, Hawasa, and North Showa
Zone primary schools) document that Special Needs Education (SNE) units often lack EthSL

===== PAGE 11 =====
textbooks and trained sign linguists, relying instead on Signed Amharic or spoken local
languages, which leaves many pre-lingually Deaf students with barriers in reading written
Amharic or English58. Furthermore, empirical studies of Deaf and hard-of-hearing students in
online learning found that presenting procedural tutorials in **sign language (or sign language
combined with captioning)** significantly improved content comprehension (p \< 0.05) compared
to other methods, and noted that non-syntactically segmented subtitles increase cognitive
load70.
* **Realistic Incorporation of EthSL into OpenPath:** While real-time 3D avatar generation for
EthSL is not yet production-ready for low-end mobile browsers (current Ethiopian computer-
vision research focuses on recognizing static Amharic alphabet signs via SVM/ANN or skeleton-
based EthSL recognition via MediaPipe Holistic58), OpenPath can realistically incorporate
EthSL through three practical mechanisms:
1. **Schema-Level Sign-Language Video Hooks (ethslVideoUrl):** Because core registration
prompts (*"Enter your full name"*, *"What is your date of birth?"*, *"Do you have a Fayda ID?"*,
*"Consent to share information"*) are finite and standardized, OpenPath's form schema can
attach short, pre-compressed WebM/MP4 video clips of a human EthSL interpreter signing each
question, toggleable via a Sign Language button next to the question heading.
2. **Linking to the AAU Digital EthSL Dictionary:** Launched in December 2023 by Addis
Ababa University with U.S. government support (https\://ethsl.aau.edu.et), this free online and
offline-downloadable PDF dictionary provides standardized EthSL signs that OpenPath can
integrate for key onboarding and employment terms71.
3. **Text/Video Relay Support & Non-Voice Authentication:** OpenPath must never require
voice phone calls for OTP verification or customer assistance; all notifications must pair visual
banners with vibration alerts, and human support escalation must offer text chat or video relay
with EthSL interpreters trained through programs like AAU's B.A. in Ethiopian Sign Language
and Deaf Culture64.
#### **C. Motor Disabilities and Limited Dexterity**
Users with motor impairments—such as hand tremors, cerebral palsy, ALS, or spinal cord
injuries—navigate forms via full keyboard, single/dual **Switch Access**, **Voice Control / Voice
Access**, or one-handed thumb touch on mobile devices37.
* **Target Size Evidence (Parhi et al., 2006 & Motor-Impaired Studies vs. WCAG 2.5.8):** While
**WCAG 2.2 SC 2.5.8 Target Size (Minimum) (Level AA)** requires interactive targets to be at
least **24×24 CSS pixels** (or have a 24 px spacing circle)36, empirical mobile HCI research
shows that larger targets are vital for thumb and motor-impaired accuracy. Parhi, Karlson, and

===== PAGE 12 =====
Bederson (2006) found that one-handed thumb use on small touchscreens requires **9.2 mm to
9.6 mm** targets to avoid degrading performance and preference39. Extending this to **motor-
impaired touchscreen users**, empirical evaluation across 7 mm, 12 mm, and 17 mm targets
found a statistically significant effect of target size on task errors (F(1,42) \= 6.56, p \< 0.01),
identifying **12 mm (\~45–48 CSS pixels)** as the optimal design commitment and revealing
significantly higher error rates in top screen targets compared to bottom targets (p \< 0.05)39.
OpenPath should therefore enforce a **minimum 48×48 CSS pixel touch target** with generous
spacing between controls (aligning with Apple/Google mobile guidelines and WCAG 2.5.5 Level
AAA), preventing a user with hand tremors on a bumpy bus from accidentally hitting "Cancel"
instead of "Submit"36.
* **Eliminating Dragging and Redundant Entry:** Per **WCAG 2.2 SC 2.5.7 Dragging
Movements**, every drag action must have a single-pointer alternative: file uploads need a
click/tap "Browse / Choose file" button, sortable lists need explicit Up and Down arrow buttons,
and range sliders should be avoided or paired with button steppers18. Per **WCAG 2.2 SC
3.3.7 Redundant Entry**, information entered in an earlier step must auto-populate or offer a
single-tap reuse checkbox64.
* **Voice Control & Switch Compatibility:** Voice Control users activate controls by speaking
their visible labels. To satisfy **WCAG 2.5.3 Label in Name**, the programmatic accessible
name (\<label\> or aria-label) must contain the exact visible text string1. For Switch Access
users, keeping pages uncluttered via VA.gov's minimal header/footer pattern reduces sequential
scanning steps4.
#### **D. Speech Disabilities**
Users with cerebral palsy, ALS, stroke-related speech differences, dysarthria, or severe
stuttering are excluded whenever an application requires spoken commands or telephone
verification20.
* **Lessons from Cboard and AAC Systems:** Our investigation of **Cboard** (cboard-
org/cboard, an open-source React browser AAC system backed by the UNICEF Innovation
Fund) alongside **TD Snap**, **Speech Assistant AAC**, and **CoughDrop** illustrates how
Augmentative and Alternative Communication (AAC) supports non-speaking users19. Cboard
displays navigable boards of pictographic symbols (such as **ARASAAC** and **Mulberry**
symbols using the **Open Board Format**) that users tap to build phrases and optionally speak
aloud via TTS19.
* **Completing Registration Without Speaking or Typing Long Responses:** When an
employment or learner onboarding form asks open-ended questions (e.g., *"Describe your past
work experience and any workplace accommodations you need"*), users who have combined

===== PAGE 13 =====
speech and motor disabilities cannot easily dictate via voice or type long paragraphs62.
OpenPath addresses this through an **Alternative Response Composer
(\<AlternativeResponseComposer /\>)**: alongside any \<Textarea /\>, users can switch to a
structured **Symbol & Phrase-Bank Selector** where tapping visual category tiles (e.g.,
\[ Tailoring & Garments \] \+ \[ 3+ Years Experience \] \+ \[ Prefers Text Communication \] \+
\[ Flexible Break Schedule \]) composes a structured bilingual response in Amharic and English
without requiring speech or extended typing19.
#### **E. Cognitive Disabilities, Intellectual Disabilities, and Learning Difficulties**
Cognitive and learning disabilities span conditions affecting memory, attention, executive
function, language processing, and intellectual development (where intellectual disability is
clinically characterized by standardized IQ scores below 70 alongside adaptive behavior
limitations and non-automatized reading decoding)2.
* **Distinguishing Simplified Presentation, Plain Language, Easy-to-Read, and Easy
Language:**
OpenPath's architecture distinguishes four complementary approaches documented in the
research literature:
1. **Simplified Presentation (Interface Streamlining):** Reducing extraneous visual clutter while
preserving core functionality. Exemplary implementations include **VA.gov's Minimal Form-Step
Template** (which replaces the full site header and footer with minimal chrome during form
steps)4 and **Apple's Assistive Access** (introduced in iOS 17 and updated through iOS 26,
which offers distilled high-contrast Grid or Row layouts, customizable essential actions, and a
prominent, consistent Back button)68.
2. **Plain Language (General Usability Standard):** Writing clear, succinct, active-voice text
with familiar words and descriptive headings, as mandated by GOV.UK, NHS.UK, VA.gov, and
**W3C COGA Objective 3**20.
3. **Easy-to-Read / Easy Read (Adapted Content with Visual Scaffolding):** A method
evaluated empirically by Karreman, van der Geest, and Buursink (2007) and Hurtado et al.
(2014) that adapts web content into short, concrete text blocks paired with supporting pictures
or symbols to improve comprehension for people with intellectual disabilities38.
4. **Easy Language (*Leichte Sprache* / *selkokieli*, Bredel & Maaß; Hansen-Schirra & Maaß,
2020):** A systematically rule-governed, modified variety of a natural language simplified across
vocabulary, syntax, and text structure79. Crucially, an empirical multiple-regression study of
students with intellectual disabilities by **Fajardo et al.** (57) found that **the number of co-
references (pronouns or terms referring back to earlier entities) accounted for 14% of the

===== PAGE 14 =====
variance in literal reading comprehension (Adjusted R² \= 0.14, p \< 0.01, negative Beta)**, while
**the number of propositions in a text negatively predicted inferential comprehension (p \<
0.01)**57. Accordingly, Easy Language rules require stating one proposition per sentence,
repeating explicit nouns instead of using pronouns, avoiding double negatives or nested
clauses, and using literal language20.
* **Why Important Legal, Identity, or Consent Instructions Must Not Be Automatically Rewritten
Without Human Review:** While recent research explores Automatic Text Adaptation (ATA)78,
general-purpose language models frequently misinterpret or hallucinate meaning in
morphologically complex languages like Amharic—where verbs fuse subject, object, and tense
into single words82. Automatically rewriting legal declarations, identity requirements, or privacy
consent clauses with an unverified LLM could alter legal rights or mislead applicants. In
OpenPath, all Easy-Read and Easy-Language adaptations for consent and identity prompts
must be **authored and reviewed by human specialists and local OPDs (such as ENAID)** and
stored deterministically in the form schema15.
* **Accessible Authentication (WCAG 2.2 SC 3.3.8):** Remembering passwords, transcribing
text CAPTCHAs, or solving puzzles are "cognitive function tests" restricted under WCAG 2.2
Level AA64. OpenPath must support password managers (autocomplete="current-password"),
copy-paste into verification fields, email/SMS one-time codes or magic links, device biometrics
(fingerprint/face unlock), and **Fayda eSignet** authentication20.
#### **F. Dyslexia and Reading Difficulties**
Around 10% of people globally experience dyslexia, which affects reading accuracy, speed, and
word decoding55. However, popular web widgets often emphasize specialized "dyslexia fonts"
over typographic parameters that have stronger empirical backing.
![][image2]
* **Evidence-Supported Interventions vs. Popular Unsubstantiated Claims:**
* **Evaluating Specialty Dyslexia Fonts (OpenDyslexic and Dyslexie):** In the eye-tracking
study *"Good Fonts for Dyslexia"* (ASSETS 2013, n=48 participants with dyslexia ages 11–50
reading 12 texts across 12 fonts), Rello and Baeza-Yates found that **OpenDyslexic (23.81 s
median / 29.17 ± 15.79 s mean) did not lead to any statistically significant improvement in
reading time over standard sans-serif fonts such as Arial (24.22 s median / 28.35 ± 12.39 s
mean) or Helvetica**50. Furthermore, in pairwise preference comparisons, participants
**significantly preferred Verdana (p \= 0.002) and Helvetica (p \= 0.020) over OpenDyslexic**,
and preferred Arial over OpenDyslexic Italic (p \= 0.050)51. A subsequent controlled study with
dyslexic elementary students by **Wery and Diliberto (2016/2017, *Annals of Dyslexia*, 67(2),

===== PAGE 15 =====
114–127)** confirmed that OpenDyslexic yielded **no improvement in reading rate or
accuracy**, and no students preferred it52. Studies on the commercial **Dyslexie** font similarly
concluded that it *"neither benefits nor impedes the reading process of children with and without
dyslexia"*55. OpenPath can offer OpenDyslexic as an *optional user toggle* for English text
(under its free SIL Open Font License), but must use clean sans-serif fonts (Arial, Verdana,
Helvetica, and Noto Sans Ethiopic for Ge'ez) as the default50.
* **Interventions Backed by Empirical Evidence:**
1. **Eliminating Italic Styles:** Rello and Baeza-Yates (2013) demonstrated that sans-serif,
monospaced, and roman font styles significantly improved reading performance over serif,
proportional, and italic fonts, with Arial Italic producing the longest reading time and highest
fixation duration (p \< 0.001 vs. Courier, Helvetica, and Arial)50. OpenPath should avoid italic
styles across all form labels, hints, and error messages.
2. **Extra-Large Letter, Word, and Line Spacing:** **Zorzi et al. (2012, *PNAS*)**
demonstrated in a large sample of Italian and French dyslexic children that **extra-large letter
spacing** substantially improved reading performance on the fly without prior training55.
Combined with **WCAG 1.4.12 Text Spacing**, OpenPath should provide adjustable letter
spacing (\>= 0.12em), word spacing (\>= 0.16em), line height (\>= 1.5 to 1.8), and paragraph
spacing (\>= 2em)53.
3. **18-Point Font Size & Constrained Line Width:** Eye-tracking research by Rello, Pielot,
Marcos, and Carlini (*"Size matters (spacing not): 18 points for a dyslexic-friendly Wikipedia"*)
recommends an **18-point (24 CSS px)** font size for web text designed for readers with
dyslexia55, paired with constrained line widths (such as GOV.UK's two-thirds screen width
layout35) and short, chunked paragraphs with bulleted lists53.
4. **Customizable Background Tint, Reading Rulers, and Text-to-Speech:** Providing an off-
white/warm background option, an optional horizontal **Reading Ruler** guide bar, and
synchronized **Text-to-Speech (TTS)** supports readers who experience visual fatigue or
phonological decoding barriers52.
## **Deliverable C: Best Practices for Accessible Onboarding and Form UX**
Designing an accessible registration or employment application workflow requires treating the
form as a structured, supportive conversation from start page to confirmation49. Drawing on the
**GOV.UK**, **NHS.UK**, **VA.gov**, **USWDS**, and **UK Home Office** design systems,
below are the evidence-based answers to all fifteen architectural questions:
### **1\. When to Use One Question Per Page vs. Multiple Questions Per Page**

===== PAGE 16 =====
* **When to Use One Question Per Page ("One Thing Per Page"):** Recommended by
GOV.UK, NHS.UK, and VA.gov as the default starting point for form design18. It is most
effective when: (a) a question requires supporting context or sensitive consideration; (b) the
answer acts as a filter question that routes users away from irrelevant branches18; (c) users
have cognitive disabilities, limited digital literacy, or use screen magnification/switch access
(reducing cognitive load to a single decision)18; or (d) validation is complex, allowing users to
handle errors for one thing at a time69. On single-question pages, setting the \<label\> or
\<legend\> inside the page \<h1\> ensures screen readers read the question only once18. (If a
complex question needs a multi-paragraph explanation first, use a statement \<h1\> like
*"Interview needs"*, place the explanation below it, and then put the specific question inside the
\<label\> or \<legend\> above the input18).
* **When Multiple Questions Should Appear on One Page:** One-question-per-page is not
always optimal. Splitting tightly coupled micro-fields across separate pages increases page-load
latency on slow mobile networks and forces repetitive "Continue" clicks. Per GOV.UK Question
Pages guidance, multiple questions should be grouped on a single page when they are closely
related parts of a single mental concept (e.g., entering "Passport number" and "Expiry date"
under a main \<h1\> of *"Passport details"* with styled sub-labels)18. In Ethiopia, this applies
directly to:
* **Ethiopian Full Name:** Given Name (ስም) \+ Father's Name (የአባት ስም) \+ Grandfather's
Name (የአያት ስም)12.
* **Ethiopian Administrative Address:** Region/City Admin \+ Zone/Sub-City \+ Woreda \+
Kebele12.
* **OpenPath's Adaptive Pacing Architecture:** OpenPath structures schemas into Chapters \-\>
QuestionSteps \-\> Fields. By default, each step renders either a single question or a tightly
coupled \<FieldGroup /\>. However, if a user enables **"Step-by-Step Focus Mode"** in the
adaptive bar, OpenPath decomposes multi-field groups into single-input screens; conversely,
desktop keyboard users can toggle **"Compact Section View"**2.
### **2\. Question Protocols and Dividing Long Forms into Sections**
Before building any form, teams should execute the **NHS.UK "Question Protocol"**33: list
every proposed field and verify (1) that the information is genuinely needed right now to deliver
the service, (2) why it is needed and who uses it, (3) how accuracy will be checked, and (4)
what happens if a user cannot provide an answer (such as removing unnecessary signature
demands or providing an *"I do not know / I'm not sure"* option)18. Necessary questions should
be ordered logically—starting with essential filter/identity questions and placing optional

===== PAGE 17 =====
questions later—and divided into clear **Chapters** (e.g., *1\. Personal Details*, *2\. Contact &
Location*, *3\. Education & Skills*, *4\. Workplace Accommodations*, *5\. Review & Submit*)33.
### **3\. Backward/Forward Navigation and Communicating Progress**
* **Navigation Controls:** Always include an explicit **Back link** at the top of the question page
(or a Back / Continue button pair at the bottom, per VA.gov) because many users do not trust
browser back buttons18. Never break native browser Back navigation; returning to a previous
page must restore the user's previous answers in the exact state they left them18. The primary
button must be explicitly labeled **Continue** (not *"Next"*), left-aligned so screen-magnifier
users do not miss it, and **never disabled (disabled attribute prohibited)**18.
* **Communicating Progress:** GOV.UK warns against complex multi-step progress bars that
list every individual question or allow random jumping across conditional steps18. Instead,
combine VA.gov's **Segmented Chapter Progress Bar** (which announces step and chapter
changes to screen readers) with a simple caption (Question 2 of 4 or Step 2 of 4: Contact
Information)18.
### **4\. Conditional Questions (Branching Logic)**
OpenPath distinguishes two branching patterns based on empirical screen-reader findings:
> 1. **Inline Single-Field Conditional Reveal:** Used *only* when selecting a radio or checkbox
option reveals a single simple input (e.g., selecting *"Phone"* reveals *"Phone number"*).
Connect the radio input to the revealed container using aria-controls (per ARIA 1.3), and **do
not use aria-expanded on \<input type="radio"\>**8.
> 2. **Filter-Question Step Routing:** Whenever a choice requires asking multiple follow-up
questions or another radio group, use a **filter question page** (69) that routes the user to a
separate Question Page upon pressing Continue, avoiding the nested-radio keyboard and
screen-reader bugs documented in GOV.UK Issue \#601091.
### **5\. Saving Progress, Resuming Interrupted Registration, and Offline Completion**

===== PAGE 18 =====
Adapting **VA.gov's SaveInProgress (Autosave) architecture** (4) for low-bandwidth Ethiopian
environments:
* **1-Second Debounced Dual-Layer Autosave:** Whenever a user modifies a field, OpenPath
saves their progress and current step ID **1 second after their last change**, and also saves
immediately if they select *"Finish this application later"*4. To support unauthenticated and
offline users in Ethiopia, OpenPath writes first to client-side **IndexedDB** (functioning 100%
offline) and syncs to the server when online.
* **Screen-Reader-Friendly Autosave Status:** Following VA.gov's \<Autosave /\> component,
display an initial notice on the first question page (*"We'll save your application on every
change"*) and render a non-intrusive status alert showing the last saved date and time4.
* **Safe Session Resumption (normalizeReturnUrl):** When a user resumes an interrupted
form, OpenPath returns them to the last step where a change was saved. If an earlier change
caused the previously saved step to become hidden by conditional logic, normalizeReturnUrl
redirects the user to the first applicable step so they are never stranded on an orphaned page4.
### **6\. Validation, Error Summaries, and Review ("Check Your Answers")**
* **Validation Timing:** Validate required fields when the user activates **Continue** (step-level
validation) and perform final validation on the review page before submission—never fire
aggressive required-field errors while the user is still typing34.
* **Error Summary \+ Inline Error Architecture:** Whenever validation fails (even for a single
error), GOV.UK and NHS.UK mandate:
1. Never clear the user's entered form values (34).
2. Prepend "Error: " to the page \<title\> so screen readers announce the error state
immediately on page update95.
3. Render an **\<ErrorSummary /\>** at the top of \<main\> with heading "There is a problem"
and move keyboard focus directly to it66.
4. Link every item in the Error Summary directly to the input with the error (or the first
erroneous field inside a multi-field Date Input) using the **exact same wording** as the inline
\<FieldError /\> placed after the question/hint text and connected via a red left border66.
5. Use concise, specific, positive phrasing incorporating the field's label: *"First name must be
35 characters or less"* or *"Date of birth must be in the past"* rather than *"Invalid input"*34.

===== PAGE 19 =====
* **Flexible Input Validation (W3C COGA 4.5.8):** Accept common format variations
automatically (e.g., spaces or hyphens in phone numbers, caseless matching, and Ge'ez
homophone folding in autocomplete)20.
* **Check-Your-Answers Review Page:** At the end of the flow, present a **Check Answers /
Review page** built with a semantic HTML description list (\<dl\>, \<dt\>, \<dd\>) divided into
chapter sections35. Only display sections relevant to the user's path, show skipped optional
fields as "Not provided", and provide a "Change" link on every row with visually hidden context
(Change \<span class="sr-only"\>date of birth\</span\>)35. When a user clicks "Change", pre-
populate their previous answer and return them **directly to the Check Answers page** upon
pressing Continue (unless their edit triggers new conditional questions)35.
### **7\. Optional vs. Required Fields, Instructions, Mobile Behavior, Personal Data Purpose,
and Consent**
* **Optional vs. Required:** Never mark required fields with asterisks (\*). Only ask essential
questions, and mark any optional field by appending (optional) to its \<label\> or \<legend\>18.
* **Presenting Instructions & Hint Text:** Keep aria-describedby hint text to a single short
sentence without line breaks, full stops, or hyperlinks (because screen readers read the entire
hint aloud on input focus and do not reliably announce clickable links inside aria-describedby)4.
Never use hover tooltips69. Place longer explanations or bulleted lists above the question
label18.
* **Explaining Why Personal Information Is Requested & Accessible Consent:** Per NHS.UK
guidance and the World Bank ID4D study on Ethiopia's Fayda ID rollout (15), answer users'
questions in context (*"Why do you want this information?"*, *"What will you do with personal
information?"*, *"What are the consequences of making one choice over another?"*) in local
languages15. Present final declarations and privacy agreements using clear, plain-language /
Easy-Read summaries on the review step before the explicit submit button (e.g., *"Submit
application"*)35.

### **3\. Synthesis of 6 Empirical Studies Involving Disabled Users**
> 1. Lazar, Allen, Kleinman, & Malarkey (2007), *International Journal of Human-Computer
Interaction*, 22(3), 247–269 (5): A time-diary study of **100 blind web users**. Participants
recorded losing an average of **30.4% of their web use time** due to frustrating barriers—
primarily confusing page layouts, poorly designed or unlabeled forms, missing alternative text
on graphics, and conflicts between screen readers and dynamic applications.
> 2. WebAIM Screen Reader User Surveys \#10 (2024, n=1,539) & \#11 (2026, n=1,780) (40):
Global surveys of screen reader users showing that **NVDA is the primary desktop screen
reader for 83.0% of respondents in Africa/Middle East (2026)** (up from 69.9% in 2024),
**88.8% find heading levels useful for navigation**, **71.6% use more than one desktop screen
reader**, **58% prefer mobile apps over mobile websites** for common tasks, and **16.7%
report multiple disabilities** (including 5.3% who are both deaf/hard-of-hearing and blind).

===== PAGE 24 =====
> 3. Rello & Baeza-Yates (2013, *ASSETS '13*, n=48 dyslexic participants) (50), Wery &
Diliberto (2016/2017, *Annals of Dyslexia*) (52), & Zorzi et al. (2012, *PNAS*) (55): Controlled
eye-tracking and reading studies with dyslexic readers. Rello and Baeza-Yates (2013) and Wery
and Diliberto (2016) found that OpenDyslexic yielded **no statistically significant improvement in
reading rate or accuracy** over standard sans-serif fonts (Arial, Helvetica, Verdana) and was
**significantly less preferred than Verdana (p \= 0.002) and Helvetica (p \= 0.020)**, whereas
italic fonts (Arial Italic) significantly degraded fixation duration and reading time (p \< 0.001).
Zorzi et al. (2012) demonstrated that **extra-large letter spacing** substantially improved
dyslexic reading performance on the fly, and Rello et al. recommended **18-point font size** for
dyslexic web text.
> 4. Karreman, van der Geest, & Buursink (2007, *JARID*, 20, 510–518) (38) & Fajardo et al.
(57): Experimental studies with adults and students with **intellectual disabilities (ID)**.
Karreman et al. demonstrated that adapting website content using Easy-to-Read guidelines
significantly improved comprehension for participants with ID. Fajardo et al. found via multiple
regression that **the number of co-references accounted for 14% of the variance in literal
comprehension (Adjusted R² \= 0.14, p \< 0.01, negative Beta)** and **the number of
propositions negatively correlated with inferential comprehension (p \< 0.01)**.
> 5. Parhi, Karlson, & Bederson (2006, *MobileHCI '06*) & Motor-Impaired Touchscreen Target
Acquisition Studies (39): Empirical studies of touchscreen target sizes. Parhi et al. (2006)
established **9.2–9.6 mm** as the minimum thumb target size, while target acquisition
experiments with **motor-impaired users** across 7, 12, and 17 mm targets found a significant
effect of target size on task errors (F(1,42) \= 6.56, p \< 0.01), identifying **12 mm (\~45–48
CSS px)** as the recommended target size and finding significantly higher error rates in top
screen areas compared to bottom areas (p \< 0.05).
> 6. Deaf Learner Comprehension Studies & Ethiopian Sign Language Studies (Wakuma, 2015;
Tsega & Belete) (58): Empirical evaluations showing that Deaf students achieve **significantly
higher content comprehension (p \< 0.05) when tutorials are delivered with Sign Language (or
Sign Language \+ Captions)** compared to non-signed methods, alongside Ethiopian
educational surveys documenting that Deaf students in primary special-needs units often face a
lack of native EthSL materials and struggle when instruction relies solely on written/spoken
Amharic.
## **Deliverable E: Comprehensive Accessible Component Inventory**
### **1\. Components That Should NOT Be Custom-Built (Use Native HTML)**

===== PAGE 25 =====
As Google Chrome's Modern Web Guidance and the GOV.UK and VA.gov design systems
emphasize, **developers must not build custom ARIA widgets when native HTML elements
already provide superior accessibility and performance**18:
* **Buttons:** Always use \<button type="button"\> or \<button type="submit"\>. Never re-
implement buttons using \<div role="button"\> or \<a\> tags25.
* **Single-Line Text, Email, Tel, and Password Inputs:** Always use native \<input\> elements
linked to \<label for="..."\>25.
* **Radio Buttons and Checkboxes:** Always use native \<input type="radio"\> and \<input
type="checkbox"\> inside \<fieldset\> and \<legend\> styled with CSS, never custom \<div
role="radio"\> elements25.
* **Simple Dropdowns (\<= 10 options, no search required):** Use native \<select\> and
\<option\>, which invoke the operating system's built-in high-contrast wheel or modal picker on
Android TalkBack and iOS VoiceOver3.
* **Key-Value Review Summaries:** Use native description lists (\<dl\>, \<dt\>, \<dd\>) per
GOV.UK's Summary List, never custom ARIA grids47.
### **2\. Desktop, Mobile, Screen Reader, and Voice Control Implementation Differences**
* **Date Inputs:** Avoid \<input type="date"\> for memorable dates like Date of Birth, because
native calendar widgets require tedious scrolling across decades on mobile and do not support
the 13-month Ethiopian Calendar. Use GOV.UK's 3-field \<fieldset\> pattern (\<input type="text"
inputmode="numeric" pattern="\[0-9\]\*"\>), which brings up the large numeric keypad on mobile
while supporting fast typing on desktop45.
* **Number Inputs:** Avoid \<input type="number"\> for identifiers (such as 16-digit Fayda FCNs
or phone numbers) because browsers strip leading zeros and accidentally increment values on
scroll. Use \<input type="text" inputmode="numeric"\> instead45.
* **Searchable Select / Autocomplete:** On desktop, keyboard users type and press Up/Down
arrows through options; on mobile touchscreens with TalkBack, users explore suggestions by
touch or swipe gestures, requiring alphagov/accessible-autocomplete's progressive
enhancement over a native \<select\> element so the form still works if JavaScript fails3.
* **Voice Control:** Every control must keep its visible label text at the start of its programmatic
accessible name (WCAG 2.5.3 Label in Name) so Voice Control / Android Voice Access users
can say *"Click Continue"* reliably1.

## **Deliverable F: Adaptive Interaction System Architecture**
Rather than bolting on a superficial "accessibility settings" overlay, OpenPath makes the **form
engine itself adaptive**2. A single underlying form state and validation tree can project into
multiple presentation and interaction modalities in real time.

===== PAGE 36 =====
![][image3]
### **Answering the 10 Architectural Questions for Adaptive Interaction**
> 1. **What Should Always Be Accessible by Default?** Core WCAG 2.2 Level AA compliance
must never require flipping a setting1. By default, every OpenPath form renders semantic HTML
(\<h1\>–\<h3\>, \<fieldset\>, \<legend\>, \<label\>, \<dl\>), full keyboard navigation, 48×48 CSS
px minimum touch targets, \>4.5:1 text contrast and \>3:1 input border contrast, a 3px high-
visibility focus indicator that is never obscured (WCAG 2.4.11), 1.4.10 Reflow down to 320 CSS
px, top-of-page \<ErrorSummary\> auto-focus, 1,400 ms debounced screen reader live
announcements, and 1-second local IndexedDB autosave4. OpenPath also honors OS-level
CSS media queries (prefers-reduced-motion: reduce, prefers-contrast: more, prefers-color-
scheme: dark, and forced-colors: active for Windows High Contrast Mode) automatically.
> 2. **What Should Be Optional?** Features that alter layout density, stream media over
metered connections, or replace standard inputs with symbol boards should be opt-in2:
* **Read-Aloud Auto-Play:** Must be opt-in so it never collides with NVDA, JAWS, or
TalkBack (WCAG 1.4.2 Audio Control)54.
* **Ethiopian Sign Language (EthSL) Video Picture-in-Picture:** Opt-in per question or
globally via the preference bar to conserve mobile data70.
* **One-Question-Per-Page Decomposition vs. Compact Section View:** Configurable step
pacing18.
* **Easy-Read & Symbol-Based Response Composer:** Opt-in cognitive and AAC
scaffolding19.
* **Reading Ruler & Dyslexia Spacing/Tint Presets:** Opt-in visual reading tools52.
> 3. **What Should Be Configurable Per User?** Font scale (100%, 125%, 150% / 18pt, 200%),
text spacing (Standard vs. Expanded Dyslexia Spacing per Zorzi et al., 2012), color theme
(High-Contrast Light, Warm Cream \#FAF8F5, High-Contrast Dark, Yellow-on-Black), question
pacing (Grouped vs. One-Thing-Per-Page), language (Amharic አማርኛ, English, Afaan Oromoo,
Tigrinya ትግርኛ), calendar mode (Ethiopian EC vs. Gregorian GC), audio read-aloud (Off, Tap-to-
Play, Auto-Play \+ Highlight), EthSL sign video (Show vs. Hide), response modality (Standard
Text/Select, Voice-Assisted, Symbol/Phrase-Bank Composer), and Assisted Mode (Completing
with a Helper)12.
> 4. **What Should Be Configurable Per Institution?** Partner institutions configure: brand
design tokens (validated so custom colors never drop below WCAG AA 4.5:1 text / 3:1 UI
contrast), required vs. optional question schemas, accepted identity document types,

===== PAGE 37 =====
webhook/API endpoints, and default regional language/calendar settings15. Institutions cannot
disable user-level accessibility overrides.
> 5. **How Can Several Preferences Coexist?** Preferences are modeled as **orthogonal CSS
Custom Properties (--op-font-scale, \--op-letter-spacing, \--op-surface-bg) and orthogonal React
slots** rather than mutually exclusive modes68. A user with both low vision and dyslexia can
simultaneously enable 150% (18pt) Font Scale \+ Expanded Letter Spacing \+ Warm Cream
Background \+ Tap-to-Hear Amharic Read-Aloud. If a user enables Auto-Play Read-Aloud,
OpenPath automatically cancels internal TTS playback immediately on any keyboard Tab or
Arrow keypress so it never talks over a screen reader54.
> 6. **How Can Users Switch Interaction Methods Without Losing Answers?** Because
OpenPath's state store (FormStateStore in IndexedDB) holds canonical data values keyed by
fieldId, all input components (\<Select /\>, \<RadioGroup /\>, \<SymbolBasedResponse /\>,
\<VoiceInput /\>) act as **stateless controlled projections** of that canonical store4. Switching
between Standard, Easy-Read, Voice, and Symbol modes re-renders the view layer against the
exact same in-memory and IndexedDB state with zero data loss.
> 7. **How Can Preferences Persist Across Sessions and Follow a User Across Integrated
Platforms?** Aligned with **W3C COGA Objective 8 (Support Adaptation and Personalization)**
and the **ISO/IEC 24751 / GPII interoperable preference profile** architecture (2), OpenPath
serializes a compact JSON preference profile (OpenPathPreferenceProfile, \<1 KB) into
localStorage/IndexedDB, a portable URL query/hash token (?op\_prefs=...) or scannable QR
code, and the user's Savanna account profile once authenticated. This allows a learner moving
from the Savanna Learning Platform to an external Employer Application Portal to carry their
exact accessibility adaptations automatically without re-configuring settings (**WCAG 3.3.7
Redundant Entry** applied to preferences)64.
> 8. **How Should Adaptive Functionality Work When JavaScript or External APIs Fail?**
Following GOV.UK and USWDS progressive enhancement principles (3), base HTML forms
render using native \<form\>, \<fieldset\>, \<legend\>, \<label\>, \<input\>, and \<select\>
elements styled with responsive CSS (rem units, @media (prefers-contrast), @media (prefers-
reduced-motion)). If external speech APIs (Addis AI / Azure) are unreachable,
\<ReadAloudController /\> plays cached pre-recorded Opus audio or local synthesis, and
\<VoiceInput /\> gracefully queues audio or yields to keyboard/touch input without blocking form
submission3.
> 9. **How Can the System Avoid Assuming a User's Disability?**
\<AccessibilityPreferenceManager /\> avoids medical or diagnostic labels (*"Blind Mode"*,
*"Intellectual Disability Mode"*) and instead presents **capability-focused, plain-language
choices** (*"Show one question at a time"*, *"Read questions out loud"*, *"Make text larger and
wider"*, *"Show Ethiopian Sign Language videos"*, *"Answer by tapping pictures and phrases"*)
so users freely choose what works for their context53.
> 10. **How Do We Prevent Accessibility Settings from Breaking Standard Keyboard and
Screen-Reader Functionality?** OpenPath enforces a **Semantic Invariance Rule**: adaptive

===== PAGE 38 =====
modes only add supportive scaffolding (larger CSS tokens, extra audio/video slots, decomposed
steps, or phrase-bank buttons) on top of standard HTML \<fieldset\>, \<legend\>, \<label\>,
\<input\>, and \<button\> elements—never stripping ARIA roles, hijacking keyboard navigation,
or blocking pinch-to-zoom25.
## **Deliverable G: Voice, Speech, and Amharic Language Investigation**
OpenPath aims to support **Amharic (am-ET)** and **English (en-US)** speech-to-text (ASR)
and text-to-speech (TTS) initially, with expansion to **Afaan Oromoo (om-ET)** and **Tigrinya
(ti-ET)**29. Our investigation highlights why empirical verification on Ethiopian languages is
mandatory before selecting a speech stack26.
### **1\. Linguistic and Technical Challenges of Amharic Speech Processing**
Amharic—the official working language of the Federal Democratic Republic of Ethiopia and the
second-largest Semitic language after Arabic—poses four critical challenges for ASR and TTS
systems27:
* **Agglutinative Morphology & Word-Boundary Spacing:** Amharic verbs fuse subject, object,
tense, and prepositions into single complex words26. Because one incorrect suffix on an
agglutinative word counts as a full word error in Word Error Rate (WER), evaluators such as
dataset.et emphasize inspecting Character Error Rate (CER) alongside WER26. Furthermore,
the peer-reviewed study *Semantically Corrected Amharic ASR* (arXiv:2404.13362v131) found
that existing Amharic ASR benchmarks often ignored word-boundary spacing; when vanilla
SpeechBrainASR, PhonemeAWav2Vec, and CharacterAWav2Vec models were evaluated on
spacing-corrected Amharic datasets, **their WER jumped from 24.9%–28.8% to
96.6%–99.9%** (14.0%–14.3% CER), whereas applying a Transformer Seq2SeqBT5 post-
correction model reduced errors to **5.5% CER and 23.3% WER**31.
* **Ge'ez Syllabary (*Fidel*), Homophones, and Punctuation:** Written in a syllabary of over 200
consonant-vowel characters, Amharic includes historical Ge'ez characters with identical modern
pronunciation (homophones), requiring homophone folding during evaluation and search26. For
TTS, engines must accept *fidel* input directly (romanized Amharic forces models to guess and
compounds errors) and treat Ge'ez punctuation (። full stop, ፣ comma) as sentence boundaries
rather than reading punctuation symbols aloud27.
* **Ejectives, Gemination, and Register:** Amharic distinguishes glottalized ejective consonants
(such as ጸ, ቀ, and ጠ), uses consonant length (gemination) to distinguish word meanings, and

===== PAGE 39 =====
differentiates formal versus everyday address (እርስዎ vs. አንተ)27. Weak handling of ejectives
and gemination is the primary giveaway of poor synthetic Amharic TTS27.
* **Telecommunication Degradation:** The TeleASR-Amharic benchmark study (143) found that
under severe telecommunication channel degradation typical of mobile voice networks, the
WER of the best-performing Amharic ASR model increased from **0.23 (23%) to 0.79 (79%)**.

### **3\. Handling Code-Switching, Names, Phone Numbers, Addresses, and Recognition
Failures**
* **Addressing Numeric and Proper-Noun Errors in Amharic TTS:** In the August 2026 Addis
Voice 2 benchmark (28), excluding digits across 87 prompts yielded **4.44% CER and 13.87%
WER**, whereas evaluating the **13 numeric-only prompts yielded 14.94% CER and 28.18%
WER** (with the full 100-prompt strict set at 5.76% CER / 15.80% WER). AmiSus's 2026
Amharic TTS guide likewise notes that synthetic voices slip most on numbers, abbreviations,
personal names, and place names, recommending spelling out numbers and abbreviations in
*fidel* and keeping sentences broken cleanly at ።27. OpenPath therefore includes a
deterministic **Amharic Number-to-Fidel Expander** in @openpath/ethiopia that converts digits
(such as phone numbers, dates, and ages) into written Amharic words before passing dynamic
strings to cloud TTS.
* **Code-Switching, Names, and Post-ASR Normalization:**
* Because open streaming ASR models like Addis Scribe Streaming had punctuation removed
from their training text and are trained on Amharic speech (137), raw outputs lack punctuation
and may struggle with code-switched English technical terms or homophone variations in
Ethiopian names and Woredas.
* Following arXiv:2404.13362v1 (31), OpenPath's backend speech pipeline applies
Seq2SeqBT5 sub-word spacing normalization for free-text dictation.
* Following dataset.et's empirical finding (26), Shook Medium and Ethio-ASR 1B are
complementary (Shook was better on 61% of recordings and Ethio-ASR on 29%, with Ethio-
ASR producing 0/500 badly wrong transcripts); keeping recordings where two models agree

===== PAGE 43 =====
within 0.10 CER cuts label error nearly in half (from 0.091 to 0.052)26. For asynchronous offline
voice recordings uploaded when connectivity returns, OpenPath can cross-check Addis Scribe
and Hohe transcripts and flag any divergence (\> 0.10 CER) for explicit user or human
review26.
* For structured fields (\<EthiopianAddressSelector /\>, \<SearchableSelect /\>), OpenPath
folds Ge'ez homophones (26) and matches the spoken utterance against the valid schema
options, presenting the top matches inside **\<AnswerConfirmation /\>** so the user confirms
with a single tap or spoken *"አዎ (Yes)"*31.

### **1\. Designing for the Ethiopian Technological, Linguistic, and Civic Context**
OpenPath's architecture incorporates six specific engineering adaptations grounded in
Ethiopian digital public-service documentation (12):
> 1. **Android-First, Low-Bandwidth, Offline-Capable Architecture:**
* Targeting budget Android smartphones (such as Android 6+ devices with 2 GB RAM96) over
intermittent mobile connections, OpenPath keeps its core JS bundle compact, avoids
downloading multi-hundred-megabyte ML models to the client browser, and persists all form
state, administrative dictionaries, and static .opus audio prompts in a **Service Worker \+
IndexedDB Offline Store**4.
* When cloud speech services are unreachable, users still hear pre-recorded .opus question
audio, read high-contrast or Easy-Read text, and complete the entire onboarding flow offline;
any recorded voice answers or document photos queue in IndexedDB for background
synchronization once connectivity returns.
> 2. **Ge'ez Script (*Fidel*) Rendering:**
* Amharic and Tigrinya use the Ge'ez syllabary (*fidel*)27. OpenPath sets generous vertical
line spacing (line-height: 1.65 default, 1.9 in Reading Assistance mode), bans font-style: italic

#### **Shadow DOM vs. Light DOM: Solving the Cross-Root ARIA Problem**
When building Web Components (\<openpath-form\>) for third-party embedding, using granular
**Shadow DOM** creates severe accessibility hazards (22):
> 1. **ID Scoping Breaks ARIA Relationships:** In Shadow DOM, element IDs are scoped to
their shadow root. Any aria-labelledby, aria-describedby, aria-controls, aria-activedescendant, or
\<label for\> attribute that points across a shadow boundary **fails silently**, leaving inputs
unlabeled or detaching error messages for screen reader users22.
> 2. **Limitations of Reflected ARIA Element Properties on Older Browsers:** While W3C
introduced ElementInternals and reflected JavaScript element references
(element.ariaLabelledByElements \= \[label\]) to bridge shadow roots, Google Chrome's official
documentation notes that these properties **only resolve outward** (towards ancestor trees, not
inward into descendant shadow roots while the Reference Target proposal remains emerging)
and only reached cross-browser support in **Chrome 135 (April 2025\) and Firefox 136 (March
2025\)** (with Safari 16.4 in March 2023\)22. Furthermore, Safari has permanently declined
customized built-in elements (extends HTMLButtonElement with is="...")25. On older Android
browsers and WebViews common in Ethiopia, cross-root ARIA reflection is unavailable.
> 3. **OpenPath's Hybrid Light-DOM \+ CSS @scope / @layer Architecture:** Following
Chrome's and Nolan Lawson's guidance (22), \<openpath-form\> renders its form controls in the
**Light DOM** (or keeps every label, hint, error, and input within the same DOM tree without
splitting components across separate shadow roots) and isolates styles using **CSS @layer

===== PAGE 49 =====
and @scope (.op-root)**22. This preserves 100% compatibility with native \<label for\>, aria-
describedby, browser password-manager autofill (WCAG 3.3.8), and automated axe-core
auditing across all Android and desktop browsers22.

## **Deliverable I: Accessibility Testing and Quality Assurance Strategy**
Passing an automated scan is never sufficient proof of accessibility131. Deque Systems' 2021
study found that automated testing with **axe-core covers roughly 57% of WCAG issues by
volume**, while simpler audits like Google Lighthouse catch only **30% to 40%** of issues9.
### **1\. What Automated Testing Cannot Detect**
Automated scanners cannot detect:
> 1. **Actual Screen-Reader Speech Output:** Only manual screen-reader testing reveals
whether dynamic state changes are actually announced—such as the alphagov/govuk-frontend
\#979 finding that **JAWS 2026, NVDA 2026.1, VoiceOver, and TalkBack on Android 17 do not
announce expanded/collapsed states on radio buttons**8.
> 2. **Live Region Timing Collisions:** Automation checks that aria-live exists, but cannot detect
whether un-debounced status updates talk over a user's typing (requiring GOV.UK's 1,400 ms
debounce65).
> 3. **Linguistic, Sensory, and Cognitive Quality:** Automation cannot verify whether Amharic
TTS mispronounces digits (28.18% WER on numeric prompts28), whether EthSL sign videos
match the question meaning, or whether Easy-Read text eliminates confusing pronoun co-
references57.

===== PAGE 57 =====
> 4. **WCAG 2.2 Flow & Pointer Criteria:** Criteria such as **2.4.11 Focus Not Obscured**,
**2.5.7 Dragging Movements**, **3.2.6 Consistent Help**, **3.3.7 Redundant Entry**, and
**3.3.8 Accessible Authentication** require manual keyboard, pointer, and flow walkthroughs36.
### **2\. Evaluation of Testing Tooling for OpenPath**
* **@axe-core/playwright (CI/CD Gate):** Runs headless browser checks across every
component and form state (initial, focused, validation error, conditional reveal, Check-Your-
Answers, 320 px mobile viewport) enforcing zero violations on WCAG 2.0, 2.1, and 2.2 AA rules
(including target-size 2.5.8)9.
* **Storybook Accessibility Addon (@storybook/addon-a11y):** Provides real-time component-

## **Final Question: What Exactly We Should Build to Win the Hackathon**
If our multidisciplinary team were building **OpenPath** from scratch with limited development
time to win an accessibility-focused hackathon—and genuinely improve access for disabled
learners and job seekers in Ethiopia—we would **not** build a generic form builder with a
microphone button. Competing teams that connect browser webkitSpeechRecognition or vanilla
OpenAI Whisper to a standard React form will struggle on Amharic (\>100% WER on vanilla
Whisper26), exclude Deaf and non-speaking applicants, and alienate rural Ethiopians who use
the Ethiopian calendar or lack standard IDs12.
Here is the exact blueprint we will build and demonstrate live:
### **1\. The Product Architecture**
We will build **OpenPath** as a **3-package TypeScript monorepo (@openpath/core,
@openpath/react, @openpath/wc) \+ a lightweight Fastify/Node backend proxy
(@openpath/server)**:
* **Single Canonical Schema, Polymorphous Rendering:** A single declarative JSON schema
(savanna-onboarding-v1.json) drives an offline-first IndexedDB state machine (SaveInProgress
with 1-second debounced autosave and normalizeReturnUrl4).
* **Dual Distribution from Day 1:** It compiles into both a native React component library
(@openpath/react) for Savanna's modern frontend and a **Light-DOM Custom Element
(\<openpath-form\>)** isolated via CSS @layer and @scope (@openpath/wc) so any legacy
